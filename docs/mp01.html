# Task 1 download and standardize the Netflix reports
load_netflix_data <- function(){
  data_dir <- path("data", "mp01")
  
  if(!dir_exists(data_dir)){
    dir_create(data_dir, showWarnings=FALSE, recurse=TRUE)
  }
  
  NETFLIX_URLS <- list(
    `2023H1` = "https://about.netflix.com/news/what-we-watched-a-netflix-engagement-report",
    `2023H2` = "https://about.netflix.com/news/what-we-watched-the-second-half-of-2023",
    `2024H1` = "https://about.netflix.com/news/what-we-watched-the-first-half-of-2024",
    `2024H2` = "https://about.netflix.com/news/what-we-watched-the-second-half-of-2024",
    `2025H1` = "https://about.netflix.com/news/what-we-watched-the-first-half-of-2025",
    `2025H2` = "https://about.netflix.com/news/what-we-watched-the-second-half-of-2025",
    `2026H1` = "https://about.netflix.com/news/what-we-watched-the-first-half-of-2026"
  )
  
  download_if_needed <- function(url, local){
    if(!file_exists(local)){
      download.file(url, local, quiet = TRUE, mode = "wb")
    }
    
    return(local)
  }
  
  NETFLIX_URLS |> 
    map(read_html) |> 
    map(html_element, xpath = "//a[contains(@href, '.xlsx')]") |> 
    map_chr(html_attr, "href") |> 
    enframe("period", "url") |> 
    mutate(path = path(data_dir, paste0(period, ".xlsx")), 
           path = map2(url, path, download_if_needed), 
           data = map(path, read_excel, skip=3, na="*"), 
           data = map(data, \(x) x |> mutate(`Release Date` = as.Date(`Release Date`)))) |>
    unnest(data) |> 
    select(-url, -path) |> 
    mutate(year=str_sub(period, 1, 4), 
           half=str_sub(period, start=6), 
           period_start = make_date(year, if_else(half==1, 1, 7), 1), 
           period_end = make_date(year, if_else(half==1, 6, 12), if_else(half==1, 30, 31))) |> 
    filter(!is.na(`Hours Viewed`)) |> 
    mutate(live_event = !is.na(Runtime) & str_detect(Runtime, "\\d+:\\d+\\*"), 
           clean_time = str_remove(Runtime, fixed("*")),
           Runtime = 60 * hour(hm(clean_time)) + minute(hm(clean_time))) |>
    select(-clean_time) |>
    mutate(Runtime = coalesce(Runtime, mean(Runtime, na.rm=TRUE)), 
           .by=Title, 
           Title = stringi::stri_trans_general(Title, "Latin-ASCII"),
           Title = str_remove(Title, " // .*"), 
           Title = str_remove(Title, "[^[:ascii:]].*"), 
           Title = str_trim(Title)) |>
    filter_out(Title == "") |>
    select(year, 
           period, 
           period_start, 
           period_end, 
           Title, 
           -half, 
           Runtime, 
           Views, 
           `Hours Viewed`, 
           `Release Date`,
           `Available Globally?`, 
           live_event)
}

NETFLIX_DATA <- load_netflix_data()

What the table above says:

The cleaned NETFLIX_DATA dataset contains 61,892 observations across 11 variables, spanning seven semiannual reporting periods from January 1, 2023, to June 30, 2026. Core fields, including title, reporting period, and hours viewed, are fully populated, while several metadata fields exhibit substantial messiness. Release date has the highest percentage of missing values at 57.0%, followed by views at 29.4% and runtime at 21.1%. These gaps reflect differences in reporting coverage and metadata availability. The dataset represents 24,427 unique titles, about 35% of these unique values are available globally, live events make up about 310 entries, which is around 1% of the dataset. Viewing metrics are strongly right-skewed: median watch time is 1.8 million hours, compared with a mean of 8.27 million, while median views are 400,000, compared with a mean of 1.38 million. This disparity indicates that most titles attract comparatively modest audiences, whereas a small number of highly popular releases generate disproportionately large viewing totals. The most-watched individual title recorded 964.1 million hours viewed, while the maximum recorded view count reached 144.8 million.

What We Can Gather from the Netflix Data

As we can see, the dataset covers a wide range of dates, beginning on January 01, 2023 and ending on June 30, 2026. It also contains 24,427 unique TV shows and movies. Beyond exploring viewing patterns, this dataset allows us to answer a variety of interesting questions about our favorite Netflix titles, including:

  • How many total hours have been spent watching Bridgerton?

    • The analysis shows that 4,862,800,000 hours were spent watching Bridgerton.

  • How many hours have been spent watching Stranger Things across all five seasons?

    • The analysis shows that 5,548,500,000 hours were spent watching all five seasons of Stranger Things.

  • What percentage of total viewing time was spent watching the top 10 titles during the first half of 2026?

    • The calculations reveal that 6.7% of total viewing time was spent watching the top 10 titles during this period.

  • What fraction of Netflix’s catalog is available globally?

    • Our results indicate that 35.1% of Netflix’s catalog is available globally which equals about 52.1% of all viewing hours that went to globally available titles.

These questions represent just a small sample of the insights we can uncover using the data provided by Netflix. By analyzing this dataset, we can better understand viewing habits, identify popular titles, and explore broader trends in Netflix consumption and content availability.

Introduction

This memo presents findings from the analysis shown above on Netflix’s viewing data. The data and charts above covers 7 semiannual reporting periods from January 2023 all the way up to June 2026. After cleaning the dataset we now have 61,892 observations across 12 variables, providing insight into viewing patterns, content availability, catalog composition, and audience engagement. The analysis helps us pinpoint opportunities to strengthen Netflix’s content strategy by examining the balance between the titles available globally and the titles that are only regionally licensed. By studying the relationship between major releases and the performance of established catalog content we can make more informed decisions based on content acquisition, licensing, production, and promotional decisions. However, viewership metrics should be considered alongside financial performance and subscriber behavior before significant investment decisions are made.

Global Versus Regional Licensed Content

The analysis found that 35% of unique titles in the dataset were available globally. This means that most titles were not listed as globally accessible. This distribution raises the question: how can Netflix balance regional licensing strategies with the possible benefits of making there content available to the global public. From this analysis we can gather that regional licensing allows Netflix to tailor its catalog to local preferences, cultural interests, and market-specific demand. At the same time, restrictions on availability may limit the potential audience for titles that could appeal to viewers across multiple markets.

Netflix should consider doing a market-level review of regional licensing performance to figure out which titles have the most potential for distribution to other regions in the world. This review should compare regional viewing hours, title popularity, licensing costs, and audience demand. Titles that demonstrate strong engagement across multiple markets should be considered for wider availability across multiple regions, if the benefits justify the additional licensing expenses. Additionally content with concentrated regional demand may remain better suited to targeted licensing strategies. This approach would allow Netflix to make evidence-based decisions about content availability instead of just assuming that global distribution is automatically more profitable. Examing these opportunities could improve content utilization, enjoyment and viewership while preserving the flexibility needed to serve distinct regional audiences.

Major Hits Versus Long-Tail Content

This analysis also shows the importance of balancing high-profile releases with established titles that continue to attract viewers over time. Across the 7 reporting periods, the leading title in each period generated between roughly 620 million and 964 million viewing hours. Even though these titles were viewed by a large number of people, this only represented about 0.9% to 1.4% of the dataset’s total viewing hours. This suggests that even major hits operate within a broader viewing environment in which audiences consume a wide range of content.

Established titles may also provide continuing value between major releases. For example, Pablo Escobar, el patrón del mal: Season 1 averaged 136 million viewing hours across the periods we looked at while its lowest reported period still reached about 105.5 million hours. Chiquititas (2013) also showed a similar pattern, averaging 120 million hours and recording at least 105 million hours. These results indicate consistent viewing performance within the dataset.

Netflix should maintain a balanced content investment strategy that supports major productions while examining the continued contribution of its current catalog. Decisions about renewals, acquisitions, promotions, and continued availability of these shows should be considered more than peak viewing hours. Relevant measures should also include production and licensing costs, viewing consistency, completion rates, subscriber engagement, and the potential contribution of a title to attracting or retaining subscribers. A structured comparison of these measures could help identify content that delivers sustained value and reduce the risk of relying too heavily on a small number of high-profile releases.

Conclusion

The findings in this analysis support 2 priorities for Netflix to maximize profit: 1. evaluate opportunities to expand the availability of regionally licensed content where commercially justified, and 2. balance investment in major releases with the continued value of the current catalog titles. Together, these priorities could help Netflix improve content allocation, reach audiences more effectively, and make better-informed decisions about its portfolio. The next step should be to combine viewership analysis with licensing and production costs, market-level performance, and subscriber outcomes to assess the financial implications of each opportunity. Although reviewing this data provides valuable evidence of audience engagement, it cannot alone determine the Return On investment. By incorporating these additional measures into the decision-making process, Netflix can develop a more extensive and sustainable content strategy.
